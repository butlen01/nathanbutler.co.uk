Turnaround

Rescuing a Business Earmarked for Closure

A loss-making Scottish unit wasn't short of revenue, it was carrying cost that had grown disproportionate to it. This is the story of fixing that first, then rebuilding the pipeline that took the unit from loss to one of the division's stronger performers.

Loss → Profit a unit earmarked for closure

Situation

I inherited a standalone Scottish business unit that was loss-making and formally earmarked for closure. It was not short of revenue. The cost carried against that revenue had grown disproportionate to it: contracts with client commitments, press spend and staff wages baked in regardless of how a site was actually performing, and a management layer that had grown heavy enough to make decisions without contributing to them. In every practical sense it was a country manager's problem in miniature. A geographically separate unit with its own cost base, its own client relationships and its own reputation in a market that knew it was struggling, a group centre that had run out of patience, and an open question about whether it had a future at all.

Approach

Diagnosing Before Cutting

The first job was understanding the contract base properly, not assuming it was uniformly bad. For every site, the question was the same: was it selling, and if not, why? Where the marketing strategy was right and stock still wasn't moving, the issue sat with the developer, which meant going back to them to negotiate on house price. Where the strategy itself was wrong, I fixed it directly. Only once that distinction was clear did I decide, contract by contract, whether to keep it, renegotiate it, or exit it, and some contracts were exited.

One Outcome Was Certain. The Other Wasn't.

The rate card needed the same scrutiny as the contracts underneath it. Holding fees where they were, with volume as thin as it was, made one outcome close to certain: the business would close. Raising fees carried real risk of its own: if it cost the little business the unit still had, the business might close anyway. But "might" and "certain" are not the same bet. Standing still guaranteed the outcome I was trying to avoid; raising fees only risked it. Framed that way, there was only ever one rational answer, so I raised the rate card, and made sure every fee earned from it could be justified against the value actually being delivered.

Rebuilding the Cost Base Site by Site

The unit had been carrying the full staff cost on every developer site, regardless of what that site could actually support: the same cost to serve applied everywhere, whatever a site could bear. I went through each one individually. On some, staff stayed on-site because the volume justified it. On others, I pulled people off-site and sold from local offices instead, in line with the Win:Sell model used elsewhere in the business. On the rest, staff remained on-site, but the contract was renegotiated so the developer carried the cost of them: the business kept management control, without carrying the cost of a presence the site alone couldn't justify.

A Management Layer That Wasn't Managing

Restructuring at that scale is a process, not a decision. I mapped the roles the business genuinely needed against what good looked like, matched existing people into that structure wherever there was a direct fit, and ran proper consultation where there was not. Some people left. Handled badly, that is where a turnaround stalls, because the people who stay are watching how the people who go are treated. Handled properly, and with the reasoning explained rather than announced, it is what allows the team that remains to believe the change is about the business rather than about them. That was never headcount removed to hit a number, and it was never presented as one.

Anchor Wins to Prove What Was Possible

With the team set, the focus moved to winning the right type of business. I set the standard for the team, then went and found the anchor developments myself. The Victoria, the regeneration of the former Victoria Hospital in Glasgow (see the case study →), was one. The sale of homes on the old football stadium site in Paisley was another. Both did double duty: proof to other developers of what the business could deliver, and proof to a team that had stopped believing it could win, of exactly how.

Making It Stick

The wins were the turning point, not the finish line. What followed was trade and coaching sessions run consistently, the Win:Sell model driven hard rather than left to adopt itself, and a structured cadence of one-to-ones and quarterly reviews put in place, the same discipline that runs the business today.

Result

The unit moved from loss to become one of the division's stronger performers, on a cost base that finally matched its revenue and a pipeline built on anchor instructions rather than hope. Three things made it durable commercially: the rate card rose and held, the contract base was profitable contract by contract rather than only in aggregate, and the fixed cost of a site presence was matched to what each individual site could actually support. Reputationally, landmark wins such as the Victoria in Glasgow proved the recovery to the market rather than only to an internal board, which is what stopped competitors treating the unit as an easy account to take. For clients, a supplier they had every reason to worry about became one they could plan around. The recovery earned promotion to Divisional Director.
