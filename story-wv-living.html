WV Living — Translating Council Bureaucracy Into a Buyer's Journey

Wolverhampton City Council needed WV Living, its own housing development company, to prove that a genuinely social project could sell like a commercial one, across shared ownership, bulk sale to private rented sector investors and the open market, on sites from Hampton Park to Daisy Bank, with a sales operation built from a blank page, with no existing function to inherit.

6 Sites Hampton Park to Daisy Bank, across Wolverhampton

Situation

Wolverhampton City Council set up WV Living as its own housing development company, because the ambition was never in question: genuinely needed homes, on sites like the former Northicote School at Hampton Park and the former Daisy Bank Community Centre in Bilston. What was in question was whether a council-led company could sell what it built like a commercial developer, not just deliver it like a public body.

A council operates on process, sign-off and public accountability, correctly so. A homebuyer operates on none of that: they want to know when they can view a plot, what their mortgage offer needs to look like, and when they get their keys. Between those two worlds sat a gap that nothing inside the council's own structure was built to close.

The ambition behind it was substantial. WV Living has built well over 600 homes across Wolverhampton, including more than 260 for shared ownership and affordable rent, with a further 500 planned over five years — a programme scale most councils never attempt directly, rather than handing it to a private developer partner and stepping back.

The Commercial Translator

The brief was not to run a sales office. It was to sit in the room where council decisions were made and translate them, in both directions, into something a buyer moving into a three-bed semi could actually use. A planning condition, a Section 106 obligation, a grant-funding requirement attached to a shared ownership plot — each one had to become a clear answer to an ordinary question, not a paragraph of policy.

That meant designing the buyer journey almost from nothing: the touchpoints from reservation to completion, the communications that kept a buyer informed without drowning them in process, and the reporting that let the council see, in commercial terms it recognised, that the model was working. None of that infrastructure existed while a housing scheme was still being run as a capital project rather than a sales operation.

Approach

Shared Ownership: Explained, Not Just Offered

Shared ownership plots — including at Hampton Park, alongside Leasowes Farm, The Glade and Vicarage View — were sold directly to buyers who needed the process explained in plain terms, not policy language. The eligibility rules, the staircasing mechanics, the funding conditions attached to a grant-supported plot: none of it meant anything to a buyer until it was translated into what it actually changed about their move, their mortgage and their monthly cost.

Bulk Sale: A Different Conversation Entirely

Sales agreed with private rented sector investors buying in volume to let ran on an entirely different commercial logic to a single family's moving day. The conversation was about yield, delivery certainty and programme, not show homes and viewing slots — a bulk buyer wanted assurance the pipeline would land on schedule, not a tour of the kitchen finish. Structuring that relationship correctly de-risked the wider scheme, giving WV Living committed volume before a single owner-occupier ever walked a plot.

Open Market: Competing on Equal Terms

The remaining homes across each site were sold on the open market, competing directly against private developers on land the council happened to own. That meant a go-to-market standard — presentation, pricing, sales process — that gave a buyer no reason to treat a WV Living home as a lesser product than a private housebuilder's, and every reason to see the social ambition behind it as a strength rather than a compromise.

Working at Two Speeds

A council sign-off cycle and a buyer's expectations run at fundamentally different speeds, and neither could be allowed to distort the other. Committee timelines, procurement rules and public accountability had to keep running exactly as they should, while a buyer chasing a mortgage offer deadline needed answers in days, not committee cycles. Managing both simultaneously meant building a layer that could absorb the council's pace on one side and translate it into commercial pace on the other, without either side ever having to compromise on what it was actually there to do.

Reporting the Council Could Actually Use

The council's own governance needed visibility that matched its own process, not a sales team's shorthand. Reporting was built to translate sales performance, viewing numbers and pipeline into language a council committee could scrutinise — the same close-to-the-detail discipline that ran through every account, adapted for an audience answerable to the public rather than to a boardroom.

Commercial Outcome

Six sites sold across three routes to market: shared ownership, bulk sale to private rented sector investors and the open market. Bulk sale gave the council committed volume before a single owner-occupier walked a plot, de-risking the wider programme.

Customer Outcome

Every buyer, whether a first-time shared owner or a family buying outright, moved through the same operation without ever feeling they were dealing with a council. Planning conditions, Section 106 obligations and grant rules translated into plain answers to ordinary questions.
