Sanctuary
01 Largest Account by Volume
c.2,500 units won, the largest account in the group by volume

Sanctuary: the largest account in the group, run as one national programme

Context

Sanctuary was the largest account in the group by volume, more completions running through it than any other client in the business, spread across sites the length of the UK. Sanctuary's own head office sat in Worcester, but its sales management teams were regional, which meant working outside my reporting line with every sales manager and sales director in the country to keep two organisations' processes aligned, and the standard I was working to wasn't mine. Sanctuary reported customer satisfaction scores straight from its buyers to its own board, and my delivery had to hold to that measure, not just to a sales number.

Approach

Won the advisory mandate first, with consultative input on site acquisition, pricing, specification and go-to-market strategy before a single sale was made, then owned the delivery: taking the site, resourcing it with my own sales team, and wrapping a sales management function around it. To run that at national scale, I built my own layer underneath it: regional sales managers recruited from within the business, each running a number of sites and reporting directly to me, effectively becoming Sanctuary's fractional Sales Director. The local agency branch network was engaged on top of that for incremental footfall, held to a deliberately stringent set of KPIs so performance could be measured and improved, not just hoped for.

Commercial Outcome

Largest account in the group by volume, retained in full through every tender cycle, with the position strengthening each round rather than being defended each round. c.2,500 units won across the relationship.

Customer Outcome

Satisfaction scored by Sanctuary's own buyers and reported to their own board. I was held to that measure rather than to a sales number, and trained my site teams to the standard their board expected rather than the minimum the contract required.

The Victoria, Glasgow
02 Landmark Win
413 Homes full masterplan for the former Victoria Infirmary

The Victoria, Glasgow: winning the anchor that turned a region around

Context

A failing Scottish business unit needed more than a leaner cost base to recover: it needed proof, in the market, that it deserved to be trusted with a client's most significant site. That proof became the former Victoria Infirmary in Glasgow, a 413-home mixed-use redevelopment, won against Savills and pursued deliberately as the anchor for the unit's recovery.

Approach

Sanctuary tested the rebuilt operation on two smaller instructions first, Love Street in Paisley and Chase Park in Ellesmere Port, both sold out completely, before instructing the Victoria itself. The pitch that won it was built to be remembered: hard-copy presentation books, emotive material on the area's history, and a delivery led with values and rehearsed until it was as tight as the material.

Commercial Outcome

The group's pitch win of the year, taken from Savills, run at breakeven in phase one as a deliberate market-position decision and renegotiated once that position was proven. It anchored the recovery of a unit that had been earmarked for closure.

Customer Outcome

135 affordable homes in phase one of a 413-home masterplan that retained the former hospital's Nightingale wards, on a site the community had a century of attachment to. The pitch led with what the redevelopment would mean for Langside before it mentioned a commercial number.

The Tobacco Warehouse, Liverpool
03 7-Year Pursuit
500 units one of the largest single instructions won

The Tobacco Warehouse: winning the world's largest brick building after seven years

Context

Liverpool's Stanley Dock Tobacco Warehouse, the world's largest brick-built building and one of the city's most filmed landmarks, was the single largest instruction the business had ever had the chance to win: 500-plus apartments, beside the site of Everton's new stadium at Bramley-Moore Dock.

Approach

Seven years staying in the room through planning, redesign and reconfiguration, handing over strategic input for free with no signed instruction, while building the trust of the Dublin-headquartered developer's own principals across repeated trips to Ireland.

Commercial Outcome

The single largest mandate the business has ever landed. 500-plus apartments, plus attachment sales income and the publicity value of being named on the city's most recognisable address.

Customer Outcome

Two entirely different buyer journeys designed to run side by side without either undermining the other: overseas investors buying a landmark asset, and domestic, mortgage-dependent buyers relying on government schemes to make an off-plan heritage purchase achievable.

Abbey Place: CBRE Global Investment Management
04 Fractional COO
650+ units across eight sites nationally

Abbey Place: the flagship of CBRE's national fund portfolio

Context

CBRE Global Investment Management deploys capital on behalf of institutional investors into real estate, and this fund's strategy was to buy housing units from developers at a discount to open market value, convert them to shared ownership and affordable rent, and hold them as high-yielding assets topped up by government grant funding. My role wasn't a sales mandate in the usual sense, closer to a fractional Chief Operating Officer for the fund's housing operations, sourcing and advising on deals before a single unit was priced or marketed.

Approach

Sourced deals directly and advised on opportunities CBRE found themselves, then owned pricing strategy, marketing strategy and product specification once a deal was approved, shaping the commercial case before it ever reached a buyer. Delivery ran nationally across eight sites, regional sales managers reporting directly to me on every scheme, headlined by a 500-unit tower at Abbey Place, Abbey Wood, and a 150-flat scheme at Bletchley, Milton Keynes sold out entirely off-plan.

Commercial Outcome

650-plus units delivered across eight sites. Every scheme performed against the model it was underwritten on, which is the only result that matters to an institutional fund: not one good sale, but a portfolio that behaves exactly as promised.

Customer Outcome

The end product was shared ownership and affordable rent. Specification and pricing were set against what those buyers could actually sustain rather than what the fund's model hoped they could, which is why Bletchley sold out entirely before a single flat was finished.

Barnes Village, Cheadle
05 Heritage Conversion
£1.35m 156 homes, Grade II listed

Barnes Village, Cheadle: building a premium brand a London developer couldn't run alone

Context

Henley Homes, a London developer, had bought Barnes Hospital, a Grade II listed Victorian landmark in Cheadle, to convert into 156 homes. What the London-based team couldn't deliver at arm's length was the immaculate, design-led local intensity the premium brand demanded.

Approach

Brought in as fractional sales director to own specification with the interior design team and run plot strategy on the ground, holding the listed building's own apartments back from early releases and sequencing every phase to price up, not down, off the last sold comparable.

Commercial Outcome

£1.35m of fee value on 156 homes, at price points South Manchester had not seen on a comparable heritage conversion, achieved through release sequencing rather than a single pricing decision.

Customer Outcome

A finished product held to a genuinely immaculate standard, from show-apartment fit-out through to the last townhouse, because the building's history was the product being sold and buyers were paying precisely for that.

WV Living
06 Local Authority
6 Sites Hampton Park to Daisy Bank, across Wolverhampton

WV Living: translating council bureaucracy into a buyer's journey

Context

Wolverhampton City Council set up WV Living as its own housing development company, and the ambition was never in question: genuinely needed homes, on sites like Hampton Park and Daisy Bank. What was in question was whether a council-led company could sell what it built like a commercial developer, across shared ownership, bulk sale to private rented sector investors and the open market, not just deliver it like a public body.

Approach

Sat in the room where council decisions were made and translated them, in both directions, into something a buyer could actually use: designing the buyer journey, the touchpoints from reservation to completion, and the reporting that let the council see the model working in commercial terms it recognised.

Commercial Outcome

Six sites sold across three routes to market: shared ownership, bulk sale to private rented sector investors and the open market. Bulk sale gave the council committed volume before a single owner-occupier walked a plot, de-risking the wider programme.

Customer Outcome

Every buyer, whether a first-time shared owner or a family buying outright, moved through the same operation without ever feeling they were dealing with a council. Planning conditions, Section 106 obligations and grant rules translated into plain answers to ordinary questions.

Octopus Real Estate
07 New Entrant
Full Lifecycle brought in pre-launch, sites from Bath to North Devon

Octopus Real Estate: the sales and compliance operation, built from scratch

Context

Octopus Real Estate is the property investment arm of Octopus Energy, a utility provider that chose to deploy capital into housing rather than energy infrastructure alone, buying developments from housebuilders and converting them into shared ownership products. Their sites run the length of the country, from Bath to North London to Northam and Bideford in the South West, and I was brought in before any of it was sales-ready, not once the model was already proven.

Approach

Set up the sales process, go-to-market strategy and branding from the ground up, then managed delivery through to building completion. Because Octopus was funding the conversions through Homes England's Capital Funding Guide, the mandate went beyond sales: understanding exactly what the guide required (affordability assessments that test whether a buyer can sustain ownership long-term, income caps of £80,000 outside London and £90,000 within it, priority for first-time buyers and armed forces personnel) and building the audit trail that proved it.

Commercial Outcome

Account retained on the same terms as the group's most established housing clients, on a national portfolio built from a blank page and run from the North West.

Customer Outcome

Affordability assessed against whether a buyer could sustain ownership long term rather than merely afford the purchase. Income caps applied correctly, priority honoured for first-time buyers and armed forces personnel, and an audit trail that proved it before Homes England ever asked.

08 Affordable Housing
760 Homes delivered in the final year

Bringing a National Affordable Housing Operation Under Divisional Leadership

Context

Affordable housing ran as a separate national team, selling shared ownership and affordable tenures to a buyer profile, on a funding model, and against a regulatory framework that had almost nothing in common with open market new build. Bringing it under divisional leadership meant absorbing a specialist operation into a general one without flattening the specialism that made it work.

Commercial Outcome

760 units delivered in the final year, added into the divisional P&L, with a client base of registered providers, local authorities and institutional funds that tenders through formal procurement and buys on delivery evidence rather than relationship. It diversified the division's revenue away from open market cyclicality at exactly the point the open market was contracting.

Customer Outcome

A buyer group that is by definition financially stretched, frequently first-time, and assessed against eligibility and affordability rules before they can buy at all. Getting that right is whether somebody can sustain the home they have just bought, and whether public money produced the outcome it was allocated for.

Company Case Studies

Not every transformation was a client relationship.

These two changed what the business itself could do: bringing a financial services offer inside the new-homes sales process, and turning two cost centres into income streams.

01 Revenue Diversification
+£560 average added per sale, at close to zero marginal cost

Countrywide Financial Services: bringing an in-house function back in-house

Context

Countrywide Financial Services, the group's own financial services arm, existed the whole time, but the new-homes division routed almost all of its mortgage and protection business to outside providers instead, a habit built up because Countrywide FS's service model had never been shaped around new-build, off-plan buyers. Every referral sent externally was income leaving a group that already had the capability to earn it.

Approach

Engaged Countrywide Financial Services' own stakeholders directly, an internal negotiation rather than a vendor one, to build a bespoke service model shaped around new-homes buyers and integrate it into the sales process itself, not bolted on as a referral afterward. Structured so the division shared in the income the change created, in return for bringing the business back in-house.

Commercial Outcome

£560 average added per sale at close to zero marginal cost, on income that had been leaving the group entirely. An internal revenue share rather than a one-off favour, which is what made it durable.

Customer Outcome

Mortgage and protection advice built around the specific rhythm of a new-build, off-plan purchase rather than a standard high street process, and offered at the point the buyer was already committing rather than chased afterwards as a referral.

02 Cost → Profit
Top 4 of 36 divisions ranked for cross-sell, four out of five

Turning Research and Marketing Into Profit Centres

Context

Market research and marketing were run as pure overhead, cost lines with no expectation of a return, at a point where every line of cost was under scrutiny. Neither function had ever been asked to pay for itself, and both were exactly the kind of spend that gets cut first when a budget tightens, a soft target twice over, in two different departments.

Approach

Changed the format the research team produced, from an internal document into something a developer would actually pay for, and started selling it directly, up to £15k a time, with training rolled out to every BDM to support the pitch. On marketing, put a modest levy on every piece of material produced on a developer's behalf, turning a cost too small to bill for individually into real, per-developer upside that scaled with however much marketing support a client actually used.

Commercial Outcome

Two cost centres turned into income lines, which protected both from the next budget round rather than making them a target for it. Four of five divisions in the group's top four of thirty-six for cross-sell.

Customer Outcome

Developers got research they valued enough to pay for and marketing built to a consistent standard, which raised the floor on how every scheme was presented to the buyers who would ultimately see it.
