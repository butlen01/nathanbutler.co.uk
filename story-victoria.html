The Victoria, Glasgow — Winning the Anchor That Turned a Region Around

A failing Scottish business unit needed more than restructuring to recover — it needed proof, in the market, that it deserved to be trusted with a client's most significant site. This is how two smaller wins became the proving ground for the pitch that won it: a 413-home redevelopment of the former Victoria Infirmary in Glasgow, taken from Savills.

413 Homes full masterplan for the former Victoria Infirmary, Langside

Situation

The Scotland business unit was already mid-turnaround by the time the Victoria became a possibility. The cost base had been rebuilt, the management layer restructured, and every contract audited for what was genuinely unsellable against what was simply mispriced or under-marketed (see the full Scotland turnaround story →). What that restructuring alone couldn't do was prove, externally, that the unit deserved to be trusted with a client's most significant instruction.

A leaner cost base convinces an internal board. It does nothing for a client deciding who gets their best site. That needed a genuine anchor win, not just better numbers — and the client whose trust mattered most was Sanctuary, the same national account already run across sites the length of the UK.

Proving the Team First

Sanctuary wasn't approached cold about its most significant Scottish site. Two smaller instructions came first, deliberately used as a test of exactly what a rebuilt Scottish operation could deliver: Love Street in Paisley, the former St Mirren football ground, and Chase Park in Ellesmere Port, a former greyhound track. Both sold out completely — Love Street's 132 homes, the majority for affordable social rent alongside a share sold through New Supply Shared Equity, and Chase Park's share of a 141-home scheme delivered alongside homes for Cheshire West and Chester Council and a private-rented-sector investor.

That wasn't incidental. It was the proof of span and control a client needs before handing over an instruction they actually care about — evidence the sales process worked at a distance, not just under close personal supervision, and evidence it worked on two very different sites, a repurposed sports ground and a repurposed industrial site, not just one favourable location.

The Victoria 413 homes, former Victoria Infirmary, Langside, Glasgow

The Pitch

The Victoria was a must-win, and the competition wasn't soft. Savills, one of the most entrenched national agents in the market, was the incumbent expectation for a site this significant — the safe choice, the name a housing association's board would never be criticised for picking. Winning meant not just matching that credibility but making the difference in ambition impossible to miss.

The pitch itself was built to be remembered rather than merely delivered. Hard-copy presentation books replaced a screen, because a document a decision-maker could hold and pass around a boardroom table outlasts a slide nobody reopens. The material itself was emotive rather than purely technical: the area's own history, the community's connection to a hospital that had shaped Langside for generations, a redevelopment framed as continuing that story rather than erasing it. The presentation was led with values — both the business's own and Sanctuary's — before a single commercial number was mentioned, because the numbers alone were never going to be what separated the pitch from Savills'. Every stakeholder in the room was drilled and rehearsed until the delivery was as tight as the material itself, nothing left to whether someone happened to be having a good day.

Testing the Market, Then Committing

Sanctuary didn't award the Victoria off the back of the pitch alone. They paused, and instructed Love Street and Chase Park first — a live test of whether the capability in the room matched the capability on paper. Both sold out. Only then was the Victoria instructed: the outcome of proof gathered on two separate sites, not persuasion gathered in one room.

Anchoring the Market, Not Just the P&L

The Victoria was run at breakeven for its first phase — a deliberate decision, not a shortfall. Taking the instruction without significant margin protected the thing that actually mattered: establishing the business as the dominant player in Glasgow's residential development sales market, a position that would generate returns for years, not just on this one contract. Terms were renegotiated once that position was proven, but the sequencing was the point — market position first, margin second.

Result

135 affordable homes delivered in phase one alone, of a 413-home masterplan retaining the former hospital's historic Nightingale wards, won against one of the most entrenched agents in the market. It became the group's pitch win of the year — the anchor instruction that both funded the Scottish unit's recovery and proved, beyond any internal number, that the turnaround was real.

The unit that had been earmarked for closure became one of the division's stronger performers, and the Victoria was the single instruction everything else pointed back to: the reason Sanctuary trusted the business with more, the reason competitors stopped assuming the Scottish operation was an easy account to take, and the result that led directly to promotion to Divisional Director. Not every anchor win looks like that in the moment. This one did within the year.
