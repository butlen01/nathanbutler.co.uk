Cost → Profit

Turning Research and Marketing Into Profit Centres

Two standalone cost centres, Market Research and Marketing, treated as necessary spend with no expectation of a return. This is how both were turned into self-funding income streams instead, and why the harder part was never the pricing.

£15,000 per research report, developer by developer

Situation

Market Research and Marketing were run as pure overhead inside the business: cost lines with no expectation of a return, in a period when every line of cost was under scrutiny. Neither function had ever been asked to justify itself commercially; both existed because the business needed them, not because either one earned its keep.

That made both an obvious target the moment budgets tightened. A cost centre with no revenue attached to it is the easiest line on a P&L to cut, and cutting either one would have weakened the sales operation they quietly supported: research that shaped where and how developers were pitched to, marketing material that helped win the pitch in the first place. The problem wasn't that the spend was wasted. It was that nobody had ever built a case for why it wasn't.

Approach

Making the Business Case

Turning a cost centre into a profit centre isn't a pricing exercise, it's a change in how a function is allowed to think about itself. Market Research had always been produced for internal use, a document that informed decisions but was never treated as something with value outside the building. Marketing material was billed nowhere, absorbed into overhead because no single piece of it was ever worth invoicing for individually. Both needed a genuine change in mandate before either could change its numbers.

The case for change had to be made twice over: to the teams themselves, who had never been measured on revenue and had every reason to be sceptical of suddenly being asked to generate it, and to the wider business, which needed to trust that charging developers for Market Research and Marketing wouldn't damage relationships built over years of providing both for free.

Market Research: Selling What Used to Be Free

Changed the format the Research team produced, from an internal briefing document into a polished, developer-facing report worth paying for, and started selling it directly, up to £15,000 a time, developer by developer. Training was rolled out to every BDM, not just on pitching the price but on using the data itself: building the kind of mature, evidence-led client conversation that stood out from a crowded field of competitors reciting the same generic pitch. A report is only worth what someone can be persuaded to pay for it, and that persuasion had to happen on the front line, not from a desk.

The report itself needed a translator between the analyst who built it and the developer who had to be persuaded to pay for it: technically sound wasn't the same as sellable, and closing that gap sat with me, not the Research team.

Not every client bought a single report and moved on. Some, Zen Housing among them, bought on a standing basis: a regular subscription to basic reports that let the relationship be billed monthly rather than report by report, with bespoke work upsold on top of it. Zen Housing alone was worth £36,000 a year on that basis, and the ongoing engagement it created ran well beyond the fee itself: £1m of downstream sales followed from staying in that conversation rather than selling a single report and disappearing.

Knowing When Not to Charge

Not every report went out priced. Some were worth more given away: where the relationship or the wider account justified it, an ex gratia report bought more goodwill than the invoice would have earned in fees. Left to individual judgement, that call would have been made differently by every BDM in the business, so I built the system that governed it instead: clear criteria for when a report was sold and when it was given, held to a standard the whole team worked from rather than decided case by case on the day.

Tracking It Through to the Sale

Once the model was proven, I extended it across the business and tied it into the CRM rollout already reshaping how the sales side worked (see the Governance Cadence story →). That meant a report's use could be followed the whole way from the pitch to a signed mandate, not just counted as a sale in isolation. Wates is the clearest example of it working: research reports kept the conversation live through the planning phase of a site in Mold, long before any instruction existed, and that sustained engagement is what ultimately won the pitch for Maes Gwern, a £1.1m mandate in combined agency and financial-services fees. The visibility changed the conversation more broadly too: conversion from pitch to mandate secured rose, and contracted value where a report had been used as leverage in the pitch reached £11m over three years, £3m of it in the first half of 2026 alone.

Marketing: The 10% Levy

Marketing material produced on a developer's behalf had always been a cost too small to bill for individually: a brochure here, a set of hoardings there, none of it worth raising an invoice over on its own. A modest levy on every piece of material solved that: small enough not to provoke resistance developer by developer, but applied consistently enough across the volume of material the business produced that it turned into real, compounding upside.

Developer-facing brochures and scheme identity were created and billed by the Marketing team, but run and signed off at local level, not by me personally: guidelines set the standard, and scheduled check-ins with Marketing kept me abreast of what was going out. What never passed without me in the room was anything B2B: national campaigns, and the company's own brochure, website and brand identity, the material that represented the business itself rather than a single scheme. Specialists did the craft either way; where I was involved personally, the job was pace, because without someone pushing the deadline, nothing shipped on time.

Protecting Both From the Next Round of Cuts

The commercial case did more than generate income. It changed how both functions were perceived internally, from spend that had to be justified every budget cycle to income lines the business had every reason to protect. Neither was ever seriously threatened with cuts again.

Result

Two cost centres turned into income lines, which protected both from the next budget round rather than making them a target for it. Research reports alone sold for up to £15,000 a time, Zen Housing's standing subscription was worth £36,000 a year before the £1m of downstream sales it produced, and Marketing's levy turned a cost too small to bill for individually into real, compounding upside. Tracked through to the sale via the CRM, contracted value where a report had been used as leverage reached £11m over three years, £3m of it in the first half of 2026 alone, with conversion from pitch to mandate rising alongside it.

Developers got research they valued enough to pay for and marketing built to a consistent standard, which raised the floor on how every scheme was presented to the buyers who would ultimately see it.
