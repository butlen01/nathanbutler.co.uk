Owning the Number in Front of the Board

Everything else on this site sits underneath one thing: I owned the budget. This is what that actually involved, past the org chart.

5 Divisions one P&L, one forecast, one accountable owner

Situation

Everything else on this page sits underneath one thing: I owned the budget. Five divisions, a national affordable housing operation, c.80 people and nine direct reports, with the annual budget, the rolling forecast and the long-term growth plan all built by me and defended upward to the group. The number itself was the same one behind every other stat on this site: divisional revenue, grown from c.£1.9m to c.£8.2m over seven years.

That is a materially different job from running a function well. A function is judged on its own output. A P&L is judged on whether the number you committed to in month one is the number you deliver in month twelve, and on whether the group can plan the rest of the business around you in the meantime. Every lever described elsewhere on this site existed because that number had to be met in a market that spent three of those years contracting.

Approach

Built the Budget From the Bottom Up

From pipeline, conversion rates and an actual cost base rather than last year plus a percentage, so the number was defensible line by line before it was ever committed. A budget you cannot explain at line level is a number you will spend the year apologising for.

No Surprises Upward

A monthly cadence of forecast, variance and action, with the variance explained before anyone had to ask about it. No surprises upward is not a courtesy. It is the thing that buys a business unit the freedom to run itself.

Accountability the Full Depth of the Structure

Nine direct reports, not accountable in the same way. Five held full P&L and budget responsibility for their own patch. Two more carried KPI and budget accountability while I managed the P&L behind them. One held P&L oversight and advised me directly rather than running a number of their own, and one was purely administrative. The cadence and the evidence base were the same for everyone; what each person was actually accountable for was sized to what they could genuinely carry, so ownership did not stop at my level and reappear as a surprise at theirs.

Working the Matrix Deliberately

Finance, legal, marketing, HR and operations all sat outside my reporting line and every one of them was a dependency. I made the case for what I needed in the terms each function was measured on rather than in mine, which is the only version of that conversation that works twice. I also kept the three-year growth plan separate from the twelve-month budget: one told the group where the division was going, the other told them what to bank. Confusing the two is how businesses over-promise and under-invest at the same time.

Whose Budget Was Actually at Risk

The pressure wasn't only on my own number. My clients carried real budget requirements of their own, tied to asset value rather than to my figures: they needed to bank millions of pounds a month, and missing what they had promised their own CFO caused problems on their side of the table long before it touched mine. Understanding their budgeting process, and being instrumental in it more often than not, became as much a part of the job as building my own. It worked both ways: help a client hit the number they had promised upward, and mine held too.

Result

Consecutive years of revenue and volume growth, delivered against budget and forecast rather than around them, through a market that contracted for three of those years. The specific credibility that buys is the freedom to be trusted with the detail rather than managed through it. A group centre that trusts a forecast stops managing the detail, which is what allowed five dispersed divisions to run at genuine pace rather than at the speed of a monthly review. Budget, forecast and long-range plan built, defended and owned: the financial discipline a board relies on rather than reviews.

That is the same relationship a country manager has with a foreign parent, and the same one a chief operating officer has with a board. The mechanics do not change with the org chart.
