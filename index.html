How I Work

Principles That Travel, Whatever the Brief.

01

Diagnose, then intervene.

I work out whether a performance issue sits in execution, incentives or operating design — then fix the level that actually moves it.

02

Deliver through influence.

I get functions that do not report to me pulling the same way. Real seniority is not the authority to command, it is the standing to align people who could say no. That trust, built over years, is what turns a fragmented matrix into a business that moves as one.

03

Decisive, and fair with it.

I make the hard call quickly, but I make it cleanly and explain it. Hesitation costs more than a difficult decision ever does, yet speed without fairness breaks trust. I move fast and I make sure people understand why.

04

Grow ownership, not dependence.

I build teams that run without needing me in the room. Leaders who make themselves indispensable are a single point of failure, not an asset. My measure of success is a team that owns its results and keeps performing whether I am there or not.

05

Make the complex simple.

I turn multi-stakeholder complexity into something everyone can act on. Complexity does not create value, clarity does. My job is to strip a tangled problem back to a direction people at every level can understand, buy into, and deliver.

06

Build engines, not heroics.

I create systems that produce results without relying on individual effort. Heroics do not scale and they do not last. I build the structures, cadence and governance that make strong performance the default rather than the exception.

07

Coach in the daily detail.

I develop people through the work itself, one conversation at a time. Development is not an annual event or an away day, it is what happens in the daily detail of real work. That is where habits form and where lasting improvement is actually made.

08

Feed every lesson back to everyone.

I treat every deal, win or loss, as a lesson the whole team needs, not just the person who lived it. Feedback gets captured and pushed back into how we work, so the same mistake is not repeated in five different offices. Kept to one person, it is wasted.

09

Keep testing the commercial case.

A decision that made commercial sense last year does not get to coast on that logic forever. I revisit the reasoning behind pricing, structure and investment on a regular cadence, not only when something breaks. The businesses that keep winning are the ones that keep testing why they do what they do.

Six Challenges. Proof in Two Forms.

Track Record & Leadership How the business was rebuilt from the inside
01 BDM Structure
£1.1bn GDV pipeline, contracts secured

A BDM Structure Built to Scale, Local to National

Situation

Business development managers were aligned purely by geography. Sensible on paper, but in practice national and regional housebuilders heard different pitches, fees and service levels from different parts of the same business, with almost no coordination with the agency network that would ultimately sell the stock. Growth depended on individual entrepreneurialism rather than a structure, which meant it could not be forecast, protected or scaled.

Result

A pipeline built on structure and reciprocity rather than luck: c.£1.1bn of GDV under contract, sourced through relationships designed to repeat. It kept producing after I stopped holding it together personally, which is the real test of whether it was ever a system at all.
02 Growth
4× divisional revenue growth, seven years

Redesigning Pay to Redesign Growth

Situation

Variable pay was tied to the revenue running through an account, not to the outcome of it. Whoever won a new mandate had every incentive to hold onto it personally — genuinely believing they could run it better — even when it should have moved on to whoever could actually deliver it.

Result

Revenue grew more than fourfold over seven years, from c.£1.9m to c.£8.2m, and it held through a cyclical market rather than riding a rising one. The behaviour changed before the revenue did, because people were finally paid for the outcome the business needed, not the one that was easiest to bank.
03 Margin Discipline
23% ROS return on sale, sustained

Holding Margin While the Market Squeezed It

Situation

UK new-build completions fell a cumulative 10.2% over three years, with the ONS reporting quarterly output contractions throughout. In a rising market the easy route to margin is to sell more, because past a certain threshold incremental revenue is largely profit. That lever barely works when the market is contracting, so the margin had to come from how the business was run, not from volume it was never going to get.

Result

A sustained 23% return on sale, held while industry-average returns were squeezed from 15% to 10% in the same market. The gap widened rather than narrowed over the period, and none of it came from selling more into a market that was not there to sell into.
04 Turnaround
Loss → Profit a unit earmarked for closure

Rescuing a Business Earmarked for Closure

Situation

A Scottish business unit was loss-making and earmarked for closure, not for lack of revenue but because the cost carried against that revenue had grown out of proportion to it. It also carried a management layer that had grown heavy, making decisions without contributing to the result. Fixing it meant asking the same honest question of every contract: genuinely unsellable, or a pricing and marketing problem still worth solving?

Result

The unit became one of the division's stronger performers, carried by a cost base that finally matched its revenue and a pipeline built on anchor instructions rather than hope. Landmark wins such as The Victoria in Glasgow proved the recovery was real, and it earned promotion to Divisional Director.
05 Revenue Diversification
Cost → Profit two cost centres turned self-funding

Turning Cost Centres Into Profit Centres

Situation

Marketing and market research were treated as pure cost centres: necessary spend with no expectation of a return. Financial services followed the same pattern from a different angle, relying on third-party providers so the income sat outside the business entirely. Three lines that only ever took money out, in a period when every cost was under scrutiny.

Result

Marketing and market research became self-funding income streams, which protected both from the next round of budget cuts, and four of five divisions ranked in the group's top four of thirty-six for cross-sell. Financial services alone added an average of £560 per sale at close to zero marginal cost, one of the cleanest routes to a higher return on sale there is.
06 Trusted Relationships
c.2,500 units c.£12.5m personal contribution, 35% new business overall

Trusted Enough to Stay in the Room

Situation

The biggest wins did not come from a single big pitch. They came from staying in relationships long after most people would have moved on, and from being trusted with national accounts that ran through office networks and managers entirely outside my formal reporting line. Retention on that scale is not a relationship skill, it is a commercial one.

Result

c.2,500 units won personally, worth c.£12.5m of contribution, with new-business wins up 35% year on year and every national account retained. The wins outlasted the deals that started them, which is the point: relationships built to repeat, not to peak in the first meeting.
07 Governance
£1.4m year-on-year rise in new-business wins, Q1

An Extra 1%, Every Day

Situation

There was no control over how data was recorded, or how it was used to move the business forward. No reliable way to know how many potential buyers had visited a site over a weekend, and no verified record of business development activity beyond trusting what people said they had done. The business ran on trust where it should have run on evidence.

Result

A £1.4m year-on-year rise in new-business wins in the first full quarter, with a single source of truth running the business in real time, from site footfall to business development activity. The same cadence still drives coaching today, which is what turns a system into a habit rather than a one-off intervention.

Sanctuary
01 Largest Account by Volume
c.2,500 units won, the largest account in the group by volume

Sanctuary — the largest account in the group, run as one national programme

Context

Sanctuary was the largest account in the group by volume, more completions running through it than any other client in the business, spread across sites the length of the UK. Sanctuary's own head office sat in Worcester, but its sales management teams were regional, which meant working outside my reporting line with every sales manager and sales director in the country to keep two organisations' processes aligned — and the standard I was working to wasn't mine. Sanctuary reported customer satisfaction scores straight from its buyers to its own board, and my delivery had to hold to that measure, not just to a sales number.

Approach

Won the advisory mandate first, with consultative input on site acquisition, pricing, specification and go-to-market strategy before a single sale was made, then owned the delivery: taking the site, resourcing it with our own sales team, and wrapping a sales management function around it. To run that at national scale, I built my own layer underneath it — regional sales managers recruited from within the business, each running a number of sites and reporting directly to me — effectively becoming Sanctuary's fractional Sales Director. The local agency branch network was engaged on top of that for incremental footfall, held to a deliberately stringent set of KPIs so performance could be measured and improved, not just hoped for.

Result

Every tender cycle made the position stronger. The depth of understanding built up — down to viewing numbers, staff attendance and sale trajectory on individual sites — meant competitors couldn't match years of minutiae with weeks of preparation, and the account was retained in full throughout. I visited every site personally, so nothing about a location's economics was ever a surprise and risk could be assessed before it became a problem, not after.

The Tobacco Warehouse conversion in Liverpool, a landmark instruction with a major independent developer and one of the largest single instructions in the account's history. The kind of opportunity that surfaces once and is easily lost to a competitor with a longer memory or more patience.

Abbey Place — CBRE Global Investment Management
04 Fractional COO
650+ units across eight sites nationally

Abbey Place — the flagship of CBRE's national fund portfolio

Context

CBRE Global Investment Management deploys capital on behalf of institutional investors into real estate, and this fund's strategy was to buy housing units from developers at a discount to open market value, convert them to shared ownership and affordable rent, and hold them as high-yielding assets topped up by government grant funding. My role wasn't a sales mandate in the usual sense, closer to a fractional Chief Operating Officer for the fund's housing operations, sourcing and advising on deals before a single unit was priced or marketed.

Approach

Sourced deals directly and advised on opportunities CBRE found themselves, then owned pricing strategy, marketing strategy and product specification once a deal was approved, shaping the commercial case before it ever reached a buyer. Delivery ran nationally across eight sites, regional sales managers reporting directly to me on every scheme, headlined by a 500-unit tower at Abbey Place, Abbey Wood, and a 150-flat scheme at Bletchley, Milton Keynes sold out entirely off-plan.

Result

Abbey Place sold 45% of its 500 units off-plan, with the remainder following within around twelve months of completion. Bletchley sold out completely before a single flat was finished. Across the portfolio, more than 650 units delivered for a client whose only real product was the yield the fund reported to its own investors.

Octopus Real Estate
07 New Entrant
Full Lifecycle brought in pre-launch, sites from Bath to North Devon

Octopus Real Estate — the sales and compliance operation, built from scratch

Context

Octopus Real Estate is the property investment arm of Octopus Energy, a utility provider that chose to deploy capital into housing rather than energy infrastructure alone, buying developments from housebuilders and converting them into shared ownership products. Their sites run the length of the country, from Bath to North London to Northam and Bideford in the South West, and I was brought in before any of it was sales-ready, not once the model was already proven.

Approach

Set up the sales process, go-to-market strategy and branding from the ground up, then managed delivery through to building completion. Because Octopus was funding the conversions through Homes England's Capital Funding Guide, the mandate went beyond sales: understanding exactly what the guide required — affordability assessments that test whether a buyer can sustain ownership long-term, income caps of £80,000 outside London and £90,000 within it, priority for first-time buyers and armed forces personnel — and building the audit trail that proved it.

Result

A national sales operation run to a standard an institutional housing provider would recognise, managed from the North West despite every site sitting hundreds of miles away. Octopus retained the account on the same terms as the group's most established housing clients, with a compliance framework in place before Homes England ever needed to ask for one.
