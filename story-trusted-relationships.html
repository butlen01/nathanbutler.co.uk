Trusted Relationships

Trusted Enough to Stay in the Room

Patience won the room. Understanding both businesses well enough to see where they genuinely fit together, and keeping the right conversations alive on both sides of the relationship, is what kept it.

c.2,500 units c.£12.5m personal contribution, 35% new business overall

Situation

The biggest wins in this track record didn't come from a single strong pitch. They came from two different kinds of trust: staying in a relationship long after most people would have moved on to easier opportunities, and being handed national accounts run through office networks and managers entirely outside the formal reporting line, with no obligation to prioritise what mattered to those accounts.

Approach

Seven Years in the Room

I pursued a landmark 500-unit conversion instruction with a major independent developer for seven years, the kind of opportunity that surfaces once, and is easy to lose to a competitor with a longer memory or more patience. I stayed in the room, kept the relationship live and the business front-of-mind, long after most people would have moved on to something that paid off faster.

Advice First, Delivery Second

National accounts like Sanctuary, Thriving (PfP), CBRE and Octopus Real Estate were held the same way every time: win the advisory mandate first, consultative B2B advice on go-to-market strategy, pricing, product mix and phasing, then own delivery, wholly accountable for the outcome. Trust built through advice is worth more, and lasts longer, than trust built through a single transaction. The same principle scaled sideways: I drove standardisation across office networks outside my direct reporting line by setting the model and targets those networks would own, not by issuing instructions they had no reason to follow.

Speaking Both Businesses' Language

Retention over years, not months, comes from something most account relationships never reach: a genuinely structural understanding of both sides of it. I didn't just learn what a client wanted. I learned how their business actually worked, where their pressures sat, what their own targets and reporting lines rewarded. I held the same depth of understanding of my own business, precisely enough to see where the two could genuinely interlock. That's the real skill in account leadership: not remembering a client's preferences, but seeing both organisations as systems and knowing exactly where they fit together.

Trickle, Not Torrent

I trained the team to manage the relationship over years, not the meeting in front of them. The instinct with a good client is to offer everything at once, early, to prove value fast. I taught the opposite: give enough to earn the next conversation, never so much that there's nothing left to bring to the one after that. A relationship that peaks in the first meeting has nowhere left to go. One paced deliberately still has reasons to keep talking years later, which is exactly how long some of these accounts ran.

Keeping the Conversation Alive on Both Sides

The relationship that mattered wasn't only the one with the client. I put as much effort into my own business, engaging the stakeholders who controlled the capacity, the pricing flexibility, the resource a long account actually needs, understanding exactly how my own structure connected to theirs, and keeping that internal conversation as live as the external one. A national account is as likely to fail from internal neglect as external competition. I made sure it never got the chance to.

Result

c.2,500 units won personally, worth c.£12.5m of contribution, with new-business wins up 35% year on year and every national account retained across the period, exclusive rights secured on two. The commercial point is not the win rate, it is the retention. In a business where every account is re-tendered, revenue you keep is worth materially more than revenue you win, because it carries no cost of sale and it compounds across cycles. The wins outlasted the deals that started them. And the clients stayed for a reason that shows up in their numbers rather than mine: on the largest account, the measure I was held to was customer satisfaction scored by their own buyers and reported straight to their own board, not a sales figure reported to mine.
