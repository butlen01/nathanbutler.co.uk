Barnes Village: Building a Premium Brand Around a Listed Hospital London Couldn't Deliver Alone

Henley Homes, a London developer based out of Battersea, had bought one of South Manchester's most recognisable landmarks. What they hadn't bought, until a fractional sales director joined the project, was the local intensity of care the brand they wanted to build actually required.

£1.35m fee value secured on 156 homes, Grade II listed, sold at record local price points

Situation

Barnes Hospital in Cheadle wasn't just another listed building. Founded in 1875 by the Victorian philanthropist Robert Barnes as a convalescent home and respiratory treatment centre, its French Gothic Revival tower had stood over the town for well over a century and closed as a hospital in 1999, becoming exactly the kind of landmark local residents feel proprietorial about: the building everyone in Cheadle has an opinion on, whatever gets done with it. Henley Homes, a developer headquartered in Battersea, London, bought the 16.5-acre site with a clear brief: convert the original Victorian hospital building into a collection of apartments, and build new houses across the remainder of the grounds, 156 homes in total.

What became apparent once the deal had completed was a gap between ambition and delivery. Henley Homes wanted the finished product to be immaculate, genuinely, unqualified, one hundred percent immaculate, specification and presentation built around interior designers rather than a standard developer showhome package. That level of hand-holding, the sustained local attention a heritage-led premium brand actually needs, wasn't something a London-based team could run at arm's length on a site 200 miles north. The gap between the brand they were trying to build and the operation on the ground is what created the instruction.

Brought In to Close the Gap

The role was fractional sales director rather than a full-time hire, embedded enough to run the commercial side of the project properly, without Henley Homes carrying a permanent Manchester-based sales leadership function they didn't otherwise need. That meant owning specification decisions alongside the interior design team, protecting the immaculate standard the brand depended on at every stage from show-apartment fit-out to the finish on the final townhouse, and being the person on the ground translating a London board's ambition into decisions made correctly, day to day, on a site they weren't standing on.

1875 Founded by Victorian philanthropist Robert Barnes as a convalescent hospital
39 Apartments Converted directly from the original Grade II listed hospital building
117 Homes New-build houses and townhouses across the remaining 16.5-acre grounds

Positioning a Premium Product South Manchester Hadn't Seen

The commercial strategy started from a decision not to price Barnes Village against the rest of Cheadle. A building with this history and this level of finish wasn't a like-for-like comparable to the new-build schemes around it, and pricing it as one would have capped it at exactly the ceiling the immaculate specification was designed to break through. Instead, the building's own story, its French Gothic tower, its Victorian scale, the fact nothing like it would ever be built again, was made the actual product being sold, with the interior specification built to match rather than undercut that positioning. It worked: price points were achieved that South Manchester's market simply hadn't seen before on a comparable scheme.

The Plot Strategy Behind the Price Points

Record prices don't happen by accident, and they don't happen by releasing every plot on day one. The apartments inside the old hospital building, the most singular product on the site, were held back from the earliest release phases rather than sold alongside the standard new-build houses, a deliberate sequencing decision that let the market absorb and re-price around the ordinary stock first, before the genuinely unique units were ever tested against it. Each subsequent release was priced up from the last sold comparable, not down to move volume, and a small number of the very best plots, the largest apartments, the townhouses with the strongest outlook over the grounds, were held back from sale entirely until demand had proven itself, rather than released at a price nobody had yet validated. That's the actual mechanism behind a record price point: not one clever number, but controlling exactly which product the market sees, and when, so every sale sets the floor for the next one rather than the ceiling.

Commercial Outcome

£1.35m of fee value on 156 homes, at price points South Manchester had not seen on a comparable heritage conversion, achieved through release sequencing rather than a single pricing decision.

Customer Outcome

A finished product held to a genuinely immaculate standard, from show-apartment fit-out through to the last townhouse, because the building's history was the product being sold and buyers were paying precisely for that.
