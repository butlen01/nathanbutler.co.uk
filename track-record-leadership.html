Track Record & Leadership

The number, and how it was built.

Thirteen commercial, operational and people systems, each rebuilt from the ground up inside a business of five divisions, c.80 people and a 250-office delivery network. Every one begins with the result, then shows the structure, the decisions and the people that produced it. The pattern matters more than any single figure. These are repeatable methods that outlasted my involvement rather than outcomes that depended on it, and none of them are specific to property.

×4 divisional revenue 23% ROS sustained £1.1bn GDV pipeline 100% account retention c.80 people, 250 offices
The Commercial Model

“Win the client mandate through consultative B2B advice on go-to-market and pricing, then own the B2C execution that delivers it, and be measured on both.” That is the operating model behind every result on this page. It applies to any business that has to win a client, deliver what it sold, and be judged on how the end customer experienced it. The sector changes. The mechanics do not.

Growth

Three different engines for winning new business: structure, incentives and formal tender.

01 Business Development
£1.1bn GDV pipeline, contracts secured

Structural Alignment: A Business Development Engine Built to Scale

Situation

Business development managers were aligned purely by geography, so national developers heard different pitches from different parts of the same business, with almost no coordination with the agency network that sold the stock. Growth depended on entrepreneurialism, not structure, so it couldn't be forecast or scaled.

Approach

Rolled out a Win:Sell model with the agency network so the more my team won, the more branches sold and the more they wanted to help win again. Set clear lanes for who approached whom, and built a land team to bring developers opportunities they didn't already have, turning a favour into a trade.

Result

A pipeline built on structure and reciprocity rather than luck: c.£1.1bn of GDV under contract. It kept producing after I stopped holding it together personally, which is the real test of whether it was ever a system at all.

02 Incentive Design
4× divisional revenue growth, seven years

Redesigning Pay to Redesign Growth

Situation

Variable pay was tied to the revenue running through an account, not its outcome. Whoever won a mandate had every incentive to hold onto it personally, genuinely believing they could run it better, even when it should have moved to whoever could actually deliver it.

Approach

Rebuilt variable pay around a mandate's milestones instead of its ongoing revenue: 25% on signature, 25% on first invoice, the balance on the home selling. Weighted 50/50 so hoarding a mandate was never rewarded, and stress-tested the structure across dozens of deal scenarios before rollout.

Result

Revenue grew more than fourfold over seven years, from c.£1.9m to c.£8.2m, holding through a cyclical market rather than riding a rising one. The behaviour changed before the revenue did, because people were finally paid for the right outcome.

03 Formal Procurement
Scored on Paper won on evaluation rather than access

Winning the Contract Before the Relationship Can Help You

Situation

A significant share of the business I was responsible for could not be won through relationships. Housing associations, local authorities, institutional funds and government programmes buy through formal procurement, scored on paper by evaluators who may never have met me.

Approach

Led the business's formal bid submissions personally, owning the commercial position, pricing and quality response rather than delegating it. Wrote to the published evaluation weightings, made a clear qualify-or-decline call early, and let operational depth built up over years do the differentiating.

Result

Substantial contract wins with housing associations, private developers, local authorities and institutional funds, and the largest account in the group retained in full through every tender cycle of the relationship.

Protecting the Margin

Holding or creating margin when the market pushed the other way.

04 Margin Discipline
23% ROS return on sale, sustained

Holding Margin While the Market Squeezed It

Situation

UK new-build completions fell a cumulative 10.2% over three years. In a rising market the easy route to margin is to sell more; that lever barely works in a contracting one, so the margin had to come from how the business was run, not from volume it was never going to get.

Approach

Stripped the cost of winning business itself, charging developers for site boards and hoarding the business had previously given away free. Pushed productivity per head hard enough to take an unfair share of a shrinking market, and held firm on fee negotiations rather than discounting to win work.

Result

A sustained 23% return on sale, held while industry-average returns were squeezed from 15% to 10% in the same market. The gap widened rather than narrowed over the period, and none of it came from selling more into a market that wasn't there.

05 Turnaround
Loss → Profit a unit earmarked for closure

Rescuing a Business Earmarked for Closure

Situation

A Scottish business unit was loss-making and earmarked for closure, not for lack of revenue but because cost had grown out of proportion to it, carrying a management layer that made decisions without contributing to the result. Fixing it meant asking the same honest question of every contract: unsellable, or a pricing problem still worth solving?

Approach

Audited every contract for profitability rather than treating the region as one problem. Raised the rate card despite thin volume, since holding fees guaranteed closure. Rebuilt the cost base site by site, and restructured a management layer that was making decisions without contributing to them.

Result

The unit became one of the division's stronger performers, carried by a cost base that finally matched its revenue and a pipeline built on anchor instructions rather than hope. Landmark wins such as The Victoria in Glasgow proved the recovery was real, earning promotion to Divisional Director.

06 Revenue Diversification
+£560 average added per sale, at close to zero marginal cost

Bringing New-Homes Business Back to an In-House Function

Situation

The group's own financial services arm existed the whole time, but outside providers had real advantages: PLC-housebuilder discipline, better reporting, real transparency. The new-homes division routed almost all of its mortgage and protection business to them instead.

Approach

Restructured the deal with CMS from the ground up: service model and reporting rebuilt to match what outside providers offered, then ran working groups on SLAs to hold that standard once it was live, not just at launch.

Result

£560 average added per sale at close to zero marginal cost, with four of five divisions ranked in the group's top four of thirty-six for cross-sell, plus direct oversight of a standard the business had only ever trusted to a third party. The harder of two wins from the same period.

07 Cost → Profit
£15k per research report, developer by developer

Turning Research and Marketing Into Profit Centres

Situation

Market Research and Marketing were run as pure overhead: necessary spend with no expectation of a return, and exactly the kind of cost line that gets cut first when a budget tightens.

Approach

Changed the Research team's output from an internal document into something a developer would pay for, selling it up to £15k a time. Put a modest levy on every piece of marketing material produced on a developer's behalf, turning a cost into real upside.

Result

Two cost centres turned into income lines, protecting both from the next budget round rather than making them a target for it. Research reports alone sold for up to £15,000 a time, with CRM-tracked leverage worth £11m over three years: the smaller, more tactical half of the same period's work.

The reporting, forecasting and accountability that make a P&L trustworthy.

08 CRM & Control
£1.4m year-on-year rise in new-business wins, Q1

An Extra 1%, Every Day

Situation

There was no control over how data was recorded or used to move the business forward. No reliable way to know how many buyers had visited a site over a weekend, and no verified record of activity beyond trusting what people said. The business ran on trust where it should have run on evidence.

Approach

Owned the CRM build end to end, then made its data the only way anyone got credit for their work, which settled the adoption problem on its own. Designed the management information that ran off that data, and refreshed the marketing collateral in parallel so the same data showed where results tracked the plan.

Result

A £1.4m year-on-year rise in new-business wins in the first full quarter, with a single source of truth running the business in real time. The same cadence still drives coaching today, which is what turns a system into a habit rather than a one-off intervention.

09 Board Discipline
5 Divisions one P&L, one forecast, one accountable owner

Owning the Number in Front of the Board

Situation

Everything else on this page sits underneath one thing: I owned the budget. Five divisions, a national affordable housing operation, c.80 people and nine direct reports, with the annual budget, forecast and growth plan all built by me and defended upward to the group.

Approach

Built the budget from the bottom up so the number was defensible before it was committed. Ran a monthly cadence of forecast, variance and action, held nine direct reports to the same cadence, and worked the matrix deliberately across finance, legal, marketing, HR and operations.

Result

Consecutive years of revenue and volume growth, delivered against budget and forecast rather than around them, through a market that contracted for three of those years. A group centre that trusts a forecast stops managing the detail, which is what let five divisions run at genuine pace.

Client and Customer

What happened once the account was won, not just how it was won.

10 Affordable Housing
760 Homes delivered in the final year

Bringing a National Affordable Housing Operation Under Divisional Leadership

Situation

Affordable housing ran as a separate national team, selling shared ownership and affordable tenures to a different buyer, funding model and regulatory framework entirely. Bringing it under divisional leadership meant absorbing a specialist operation into a general one without flattening the specialism that made it work.

Approach

A small team, under ten people, already delivering but underselling its potential. Grew affordable housing from 10% to 35% of the division's revenue, a deliberate ceiling: other divisions let it drift toward 70% and won less new business elsewhere. The team still delivered close to 45% of the group's national affordable housing revenue.

Result

760 units delivered in the final year, added into the divisional P&L, with a client base that tenders through formal procurement and buys on delivery evidence rather than relationship. It diversified the division's revenue away from open market cyclicality exactly as the open market was contracting.

11 Trusted Relationships
c.4,500 units c.£22.5m won personally

Trusted Enough to Stay in the Room

Situation

The biggest wins did not come from a single big pitch. They came from staying in relationships long after most people would have moved on, and from being trusted with national accounts that ran through office networks entirely outside my formal reporting line. Retention at that scale is a commercial skill, not a relationship one.

Approach

Pursued the Tobacco Warehouse, a landmark 500-unit Liverpool conversion, for seven years, while holding accounts like Sanctuary, CBRE and Octopus Real Estate through consultative advice first, then delivery. Built a structural understanding of both businesses, and kept the relationship alive on both sides, not just the client-facing one.

Result

c.4,500 units won personally across seven years, worth c.£22.5m in fee value. New-business wins rose 35% year on year and every national account was retained. The wins outlasted the deals that started them, which is the point: relationships built to repeat, not to peak in the first meeting.

12 Customer Outcomes
Board-Reported client-scored, on the largest account

The Customer Was the Product

Situation

On the largest account in the group, I was not primarily measured on sales. Sanctuary surveyed its own buyers and reported those scores directly to its own board, so a weak month on one site became a line in a report the client's board read every quarter.

Approach

Trained site teams to the standard a client's board expected rather than the contract's minimum, and designed buyer journeys from nothing on council-owned developments. Built compliance around the customer, not just the client, and held delivery standards across an agency network of c.250 offices outside my reporting line.

Result

Every national account retained. A 150-flat scheme sold out entirely off-plan and a 500-unit tower sold 45% off-plan with the remainder inside twelve months, the cleanest evidence that pricing and specification were right for the buyer.

People and Capability

How the leadership team itself was built.

13 Building the Bench
5 of 6 senior roles filled from within

Grow What You Can, Buy What You Can't

Situation

Regional leadership in this business is specialised, dispersed and hard to hire at level. The market for somebody who understands a developer client, a 250-office network and a P&L at the same time is thin and slow, and every senior vacancy filled externally costs months of momentum.

Approach

Built a five-rung ladder inside one discipline so somebody was always one step below every role that mattered. Gave people on progression plans oversight of live projects, buddied directors to one another for peer mentoring, and bought the one capability the business couldn't grow internally: a Land Director who had been my client for ten years.

Result

Five of six senior roles filled from inside, including two Residential Development Directors who started as business development managers. The Land Director was a deliberate increase in headcount, changing the unit economics too, and several people I developed left, reached Sales Director level with developers, and put business my way.
