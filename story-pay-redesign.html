Redesigning Pay to Redesign Growth

A pay model built on revenue rewards holding on. This is the story of redesigning incentives so that letting go was the profitable choice, without ever paying out for a mandate that was never going to work.

Situation

For years, variable pay was tied to the revenue running through an account rather than the outcome of it. That sounds reasonable until you follow the incentive. Once someone won a mandate, their income depended on that revenue continuing to flow through them personally, so when the moment came to move delivery to whoever could actually run it best, they had every reason to resist. Sincerely, not obstructively: the pay structure gave them every reason to believe they could do it better themselves, and every reason not to find out otherwise. The cost never showed up as a failure to win business. It showed up as growth capped at whatever a handful of people could personally hold onto, a ceiling built into the pay model rather than into the market or the team.

Approach

Paying the Milestones, Not the Revenue

I rebuilt variable pay from the ground up around the actual lifecycle of a mandate rather than the revenue it eventually produced. Twenty-five per cent on signature, once due diligence was complete. Twenty-five per cent when the first invoice cleared. The remaining fifty per cent only when the home itself sold. Pay stopped following the money through the business and started following the moments that actually mattered.

Why Fifty-Fifty, and Not Some Other Number

The split itself was the entire design problem, and it had to survive one specific test: it could not reward winning a bad mandate. Front-load the payments too heavily and you'd be paying people well for instructions that were never going to sell, rewarding failure, in effect, just dressed up as new business. So half the reward sat on outcomes the BDM directly controlled: winning the mandate, clearing due diligence, getting the first invoice out, because that's exactly the behaviour growth needed more of, and it's controllable, so it was fair to reward it early and with confidence. The other half stayed tied to the sale itself, which depends on none of that being enough on its own: the meetings, the shared sales strategy, the quality of the project set-up, the coordination with branch and regional managers that actually gets a home over the line. Someone could bank the first half quickly. They could only bank all of it by staying properly engaged until the account actually delivered.

Modelled Before It Was Trusted

None of this could be rolled out on instinct. A pay-model change touches every deal in flight, at every stage, and the business had to know, not hope, that it could sustainably fund the new structure regardless of deal size, timing, or how quickly a home actually sold. I built the financial modelling for the redesign across dozens of scenarios before a single contract moved onto it: fast sales and slow ones, small mandates and large ones, clusters of deals landing at once and quiet quarters where they didn't. Only once the numbers held up under all of that did it go live.

Result

Divisional revenue grew more than fourfold over seven years, c.£1.9m to c.£8.2m, and held through a cyclical market rather than riding a rising one. Three things matter commercially about how that was done. It was cost-neutral, funded entirely from the existing variable pay envelope with no increase to the cost of sale. It was modelled before it was trusted, stress-tested across dozens of deal scenarios so the business knew rather than hoped it could fund the structure at any mix of deal size and timing. And it never paid out for a mandate that was never going to sell, because half the reward stayed tied to the home actually completing. The behaviour changed before the revenue did. Mandates started moving to whoever could deliver them within days rather than being held for quarters, which shortened time to revenue on every new win and meant the person running a client's account was the person best placed to run it. The structure is still in use, largely unchanged, years later.
