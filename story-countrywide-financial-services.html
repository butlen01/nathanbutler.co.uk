Revenue Diversification

Bringing New-Homes Business Back to an In-House Function

Countrywide Mortgage Services (CMS), the group's own financial services arm, existed the whole time, but the new-homes division routed almost all of its mortgage and protection business to outside providers instead. This is how that referral stream was brought back in-house, and why the harder part wasn't the client at all: it was persuading a function that didn't report to me.

+£560 average added per sale, at close to zero marginal cost

Situation

CMS, the group's own financial services arm, existed the whole time. It wasn't a capability the business needed to build or a provider it needed to find: it already sat inside the same organisation, under a different part of the matrix. And yet the new-homes division routed almost all of its mortgage and protection business to outside providers instead.

The reason wasn't inertia. CMS's existing model had grown up around a different kind of buyer and a different kind of transaction, and it had never been reshaped around new-build, off-plan purchases, the specific rhythm of reservation, exchange and completion a new-homes sale runs on. Every referral sent externally instead was income leaving a group that already had the capability to earn it, just not yet in a form the new-homes business could actually use.

Approach

Winning Trust Inside the Matrix

This wasn't a vendor negotiation. CMS had its own leadership, its own priorities, and no reporting line to the new-homes division asking for change: the same structural problem that ran through every account built on influence rather than authority, except this time the account was internal. Nobody could be instructed to build a bespoke service model for a division that had, until now, sent them almost no business.

The case had to be made in commercial terms CMS's own leadership would recognise: not a favour, but an opportunity: an entire referral stream that had been leaving the group for years, available to bring in-house if the service model changed to fit it. That reframing, from a request to an opportunity, is what actually moved the conversation.

Understanding Why the Referrals Left

The perception wasn't wrong to start with. Outside providers were built around PLC housebuilders: strict discipline from offer acceptance through to exchange, a rigorous qualification process on every buyer, better reporting and real transparency. What that discipline actually cost the group was the income itself, leaving for a standard the business could build in-house just as well.

Building a Service Model That Actually Fit

Restructured the deal with CMS from the ground up: the service model, the reporting, the transparency, rebuilt to match the standard outside providers had set. Working groups ran on SLAs to hold that standard once it was live, not just at launch. Then integrated the offer directly into the sales process itself, raised at the point a buyer was already committing, not chased afterwards as a separate referral.

Telling a Breach From a Recording Lag

Most of what looked like an SLA breach on mortgage-offer timelines wasn't a service failure at all, once I looked properly. It was a recording problem: offers were being logged manually, and the lag in that showed up in the data as CMS missing a deadline it had actually hit. I pushed for the system to pull live information directly rather than rely on someone keying it in after the fact. That single change did more than tidy up the reporting. It cut the noise from false breaches out of the working groups' time, so the focus stayed on the SLA breaches that were real.

Structuring the Incentive

Structured so the division shared in the income the change created: an internal revenue share rather than a one-off favour, which is what made the change durable rather than a single win that faded once the goodwill did.

Result

£560 average added per sale at close to zero marginal cost, on income that had been leaving the group entirely, with four of five divisions ranked in the group's top four of thirty-six for cross-sell, and conversion from house sale to mortgage sign-up averaging 35% across my divisions, reaching 43% in the strongest, against an 18% average across the group. An internal revenue share rather than a one-off favour, which is what made it durable, plus direct oversight of a service standard the business had only ever trusted to a third party before.

Mortgage and protection advice built around the specific rhythm of a new-build, off-plan purchase rather than a standard high street process, and offered at the point the buyer was already committing rather than chased afterwards as a referral. The customer outcome and the commercial one were the same decision.
