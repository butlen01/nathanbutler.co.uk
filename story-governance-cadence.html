Governance

An Extra 1%, Every Day

The business ran on trust, not data: a site's foot traffic unknown, a claimed mandate unverifiable. This is the story of replacing that trust economy with a single source of truth, and building a business around what it made possible.

£1.4m year-on-year rise in new-business wins, Q1

Situation

There was no control over how data was recorded, or how it was used to move the business forward. On the sales side, there was no way to know how many potential buyers had visited a site over a weekend, which meant no way to have an informed conversation with the sales team about turning that interest into a sale. On the business-development side, it was more basic still: no verified record of names, phone numbers, or conversations. When someone said they had a mandate, the only option was to trust them.

Approach

Owning the System End to End

I didn't hand this off to be someone else's project. I was involved in procuring the CRM system, negotiating the contract, designing how it actually worked, training every team on it, and running its ongoing use once it was live, the same person accountable for it at every stage, not a different owner handing it down the line. That end-to-end ownership was itself part of the wider operating model: one accountable owner for a system, not a handover chain that dilutes responsibility at every step.

Making the CRM the Only Way to Get Credit

Some people resisted using it, right up until the CRM's data became the business's one source of truth. Once it was, the incentive flipped on its own: if the work wasn't in the CRM, the credit for it wasn't either. Adoption stopped being a policy people could quietly ignore, and became something in their own interest to get right.

From Data to Decisions

Once the data was reliable, I designed the management information that ran off it, and the governance that wrapped around it: activity-point targets that gave the team something concrete to aim at day to day, not just a number to hit by month end. For the first time, the business could be seen in real time: real-time data, real-time management decisions, real-time strategic calls. And because the data was specific, coaching could be too, built on the actual evidence of where someone's activity was or wasn't converting, not on instinct.

Collateral Built for a B2B Audience With Consumer Instincts

At the same time, a genuinely separate piece of work, not a continuation of the CRM build, I engaged the marketing team and fully refreshed the collateral supporting the sales teams. Most people would have seen two unrelated projects running in parallel. I saw one relationship: get the data right, and the same discipline behind it would make everything built around it work harder too. What existed was serviceable but sterile: the kind of material that could have come from any institution, when the business itself was a B2B offering selling mandates to developer clients who cared intensely about how their own customers perceived them. I produced brochures, sales packs and presentations that were professional but genuinely warm, and trained them out to give the whole business a standard to work from. They stayed adaptable by design, so the standard never came at the cost of the entrepreneurial flair some of the best people brought to a pitch.

The two pieces worked as one system, even running as separate initiatives at the same time. The standard raised the floor for everyone who needed structure to follow. The entrepreneurial were still free to be entrepreneurial. And because every pitch, every conversation, every mandate now ran through the CRM regardless of style, I could see exactly where an individual's results tracked the plan and where they didn't, and coach the gap specifically, rather than guess at it.

Result

A £1.4m year-on-year rise in new-business wins in the first full quarter, with a single source of truth running the business in real time, from a site's weekend footfall to a business development manager's claimed mandate. The wider commercial value was forecasting. For the first time the division could commit to a number at budget and defend it monthly with evidence rather than assertion, which is what changes the conversation with a group centre from explanation to management. It also made coaching specific: I could see exactly where an individual's activity was and was not converting, across dispersed teams I could not physically be with, and address the gap rather than guess at it. I owned the build end to end, from procurement and contract negotiation through design, training and ongoing use, so there was one accountable owner rather than a handover chain. The same cadence still drives coaching today, which is what turns a system into a habit rather than a one-off intervention.
