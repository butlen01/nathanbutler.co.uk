Redesigning Conveyancing Around a Developer's Deadline

A residential developer expected a buyer to be moved in, or legally committed, within 28 days of an offer being accepted. The in-house conveyancing process, part offshored, part dependent on who happened to be in that week, couldn't get close. This is how I built a route around it without the business ever losing the referral, and without the buyer ever losing their right to choose.

c.45% conveyancing penetration reached on the business

Situation

My business unit sat inside a much larger estate agency network, taking B2B mandates from residential developers and converting them into B2C exits, either through an estate agency office or through direct sales. That put two obligations on me at once: generate revenue for the business I worked for, and represent the developer clients whose mandates I held, clients who could walk the mandate if I didn't deliver.

Those two obligations collided directly on conveyancing. The business wanted cross-sold conveyancing attached to every house sale. My developer clients expected something stricter: a buyer moved in, or legally committed through exchange, within 28 days of accepting an offer. The in-house conveyancing team couldn't meet that. Cases went offshore to India for the paperwork, then came back to the UK to be ratified by named individuals, and ratifying a case depended on knowing who on a short list wasn't on leave that week. It was a process built for the ordinary pace of the open market, not for a developer's completion clock.

Approach

Building a Panel That Could Actually Move

I went to solicitor firms I already had a working relationship with and proposed a different way of running conveyancing on a new-homes site: pre-conveyance the site once, as a single package covering title, planning and the standard enquiries, so every unit sold on that site afterwards moved through a conveyancer who already knew the answers to the questions that normally slow a transaction down. A hundred-unit site stopped meaning a hundred separate conveyancing problems and started meaning one.

Making It a Formal Instruction, Not a Favour

I agreed a referral fee arrangement with the firm and had it added to the internal panel properly, so every referral ran through a recognised process rather than an informal relationship. It started at £165 a unit. Once volume proved the model, I renegotiated it to £300 a unit.

Finding Where the Incentive Scheme Had a Gap

The office incentive scheme measured a branch's conveyancing penetration against its open-market sales, with new-homes transactions excluded from that calculation entirely. A new-homes conveyancing referral still earned a branch full credit against its own incentive scheme, without ever counting against the penetration target that scheme was built to move. Once business development managers and their directors understood that, referring into the panel was close to a free decision for them, and it's a large part of why penetration kept climbing once the structure was in place.

Protecting Choice While Still Driving It

The one thing I wouldn't do was make the panel solicitor a condition of the sale: that's conditional selling, and it isn't how I run an account. Instead, I had the developer contribute toward the buyer's legal fees if they chose to use the panel solicitor. The buyer kept a genuinely free choice. They just had a real reason to make the one that also worked for the pipeline.

Running It, Not Just Setting It Up

Weekly calls with a senior lawyer at the firm, reviewing the live caseload against the developer's own completion expectations, kept this a managed pipeline rather than a referral arrangement that ran itself once and was never checked again.

Result

Conveyancing penetration reached c.45% of the business, a new revenue line generated on top of an existing operating model rather than by disrupting it. That distinction mattered more than the number itself: none of this could run without the developer's agreement to it, and if the service had been in place but the developer wasn't satisfied, the mandate itself was what was at risk, not just the conveyancing attachment. The referral fee renegotiated from £165 to £300 a unit as volume proved the model out. Developer completion timelines held, on a process the in-house conveyancing team was never going to meet on its own.
