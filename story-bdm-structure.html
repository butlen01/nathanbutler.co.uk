Business Development

Structural Alignment: A Business Development Engine Built to Scale

Thirty business development managers. Thirty-eight estate agency regions I didn't manage. Five new-homes divisions I did. This is how those three worlds were aligned into one structure, and one £1.1bn pipeline.

£1.1bn GDV pipeline, contracts secured

Situation

Thirty business development managers covered thirty-eight estate agency regions, and the model had a hole in it. National and regional housebuilders operating across several patches heard different pitches, different fees and materially different service levels from the same company, which cost credibility on exactly the accounts worth most. Underneath that sat a bigger gap: my BDMs and the estate agency regions that would ultimately sell the stock had no formal alignment and no shared reporting line. Growth depended on how entrepreneurial an individual happened to be that quarter, which meant it could not be forecast, could not be protected when someone left, and could not be scaled. For a business setting a budget, that is the real cost. Not lost revenue, but revenue nobody can responsibly commit to.

5 New-homes divisions: the business that did report to me

The Leadership Challenge

The real difficulty wasn't designing a better process. It was that the two populations who needed to work as one, my BDMs and the estate agency regional managers, sat in entirely different parts of the business, under entirely different leadership, with no shared reporting line to fall back on. I couldn't instruct thirty-eight regional managers to prioritise my BDMs. I had to make it worth their while to choose to.

So I aligned the two structures directly, though never as a rigid one-to-one grid. A dedicated BDM covered every regional manager's patch, so no region ever shared a BDM with another, but a single BDM might hold two regional managers' patches where seniority and patch volume justified it: a junior BDM typically ran around six branches, a mature BDM up to twelve. That was the territory design underneath everything else: coverage sized to what each person could actually carry, with a shared incentive to grow the same ground together. The alignment itself, not a memo and not a mandate, is what made the rest of the structure work.

Approach

Skin in the Game, on Paper

The pairing model didn't run itself cleanly from day one. Wherever a patch underperformed, the instinct on both sides was to blame the other: not winning enough from my side, not selling it well enough from the agency side. That pattern got bridged with documentation, not discipline: sensible handover documents at every stage that signposted exactly who owned what, so both sides kept skin in the game rather than a convenient excuse when a number came in short.

The Win:Sell Model

I built the commercial logic that made the alignment worth it. My team won the instruction; their branch sold it, off the back of buyers and sellers their agents were already meeting every day, an indirect channel to market I could never have replicated by building my own network from nothing. The incentive ran both ways: the more my team won, the more stock they had to sell; the more they sold, the more they wanted to help win the next one. It was cyclical in a bigger sense too: a developer happy with how a site had sold was a developer more likely to hand over the next instruction, in that location or another, a continuous virtuous circle rather than a series of one-off wins. On the largest accounts, dedicated on-site resource carried the heavy lifting directly with the client day to day, but that resource was line managed by my own team throughout: recruited, trained and performance-managed by my BDMs and Directors, who drove the sales and customer satisfaction result behind them. Client dialogue at the level that mattered sat with my BDMs, Directors and me more than with on-site staff, though always on a transparent basis: nothing to hide, so a client was free to speak to whoever was actually dealing with their project. League tables made the results visible, and visibility did the rest: branches that had never proactively passed on a lead started doing it on their own initiative.

Lanes, Not Chaos

Alongside the alignment, I set clear rules for who approached whom, the coverage model that made thirty individual BDM relationships behave like one business. National PLC developers, whose regional boundaries usually crossed several of my BDMs' patches, were coordinated centrally, approached Regional Sales Director to Sales Director, timed around their half-year and full-year reporting, so the business spoke to them with one voice instead of several. SME developers were owned directly by the local BDM who knew them best. The handful of true national relationships came to me or my direct reports personally. Wherever an account sat, the standard of service behind it was now the same.

A Land Team, Not a Land Grab

The old approach to winning an instruction was, in effect, a generic offer to sell houses, nothing to distinguish it from any other agent, a relationship sell that depended on luck or persistence. I built a small land team whose only job was sourcing land opportunities and bringing them to preferred developer partners before anyone else could. Bringing a developer an opportunity they didn't already have changes the entire conversation: it's no longer a favour I'm asking for, it's one I'm owed. That reciprocity is what won the sales-agency appointment on the homes eventually built there, and it opened cross-sales the old cold-pitch approach never could.

Built the Layer, Rather Than Hired It

Regional sales managers on the largest accounts were recruited from inside the business and grown into the role, each running a number of sites and reporting directly to me. That was deliberate. Internal promotion gave me people who already understood the operating model, cut ramp time to near zero, and created a visible progression route that made the roles underneath easier to fill and easier to keep. Building a management layer is slower than stretching one person across dozens of live sites. It is also the only version that survives the person who built it.

Result

c.£1.1bn of GDV under contract across a 4,000-unit pipeline, built through thirty designed relationships across thirty-eight regions rather than thirty individual efforts. The commercial effect was not only volume. Pipeline became forecastable for the first time, which changed what the division could credibly commit to at budget: a number solid enough ahead of time to justify investing in the team and the land pipeline with confidence, rather than reacting to whatever business happened to land that quarter. The cost of winning fell too, because an agency network of c.250 offices started generating leads it had never previously bothered to pass on. It kept producing after I stopped holding it together personally, which is the only real test of whether it was ever a system. For the client, the change was just as visible: a national housebuilder now dealt with one commercial position and one service standard wherever they operated, rather than negotiating separately with four parts of the same company.
