BDM Structure

A BDM Structure Built to Scale, Local to National

Forty business development managers. Forty estate agency regions I didn't manage. Five new-homes divisions I did. This is how those three worlds were aligned into one structure, and one £1.1bn pipeline.

£1.1bn GDV pipeline, contracts secured

Situation

Forty business development managers were aligned purely by geography, and the model had a hole in it. National and regional housebuilders operating across several patches heard different pitches, different fees and materially different service levels from the same company, which cost credibility on exactly the accounts worth most. Underneath that sat a bigger gap: my BDMs and the forty estate agency regions that would ultimately sell the stock had no formal alignment and no shared reporting line. Growth depended on how entrepreneurial an individual happened to be that quarter, which meant it could not be forecast, could not be protected when someone left, and could not be scaled. For a business setting a budget, that is the real cost. Not lost revenue, but revenue nobody can responsibly commit to.

40 Business development managers, one for every agency region
40 Estate agency regions: entirely outside my reporting line
5 New-homes divisions: the business that did report to me

The Leadership Challenge

The real difficulty wasn't designing a better process. It was that the two populations who needed to work as one, my BDMs and the estate agency regional managers, sat in entirely different parts of the business, under entirely different leadership, with no shared reporting line to fall back on. I couldn't instruct forty regional managers to prioritise my BDMs. I had to make it worth their while to choose to.

So I aligned the two structures directly: every BDM was paired one-to-one with the agency regional manager covering the same patch, forty pairings in total, each one a genuine business relationship rather than a geographic coincidence. That pairing was the territory design underneath everything else: two people, two functions, one shared incentive to grow the same patch together. The pairing itself, not a memo and not a mandate, is what made the rest of the structure work.

Approach

The Win:Sell Model

I built the commercial logic that made the pairing worth it. My team won the instruction; their branch sold it, off the back of buyers and sellers their agents were already meeting every day, an indirect channel to market I could never have replicated by building my own network from nothing. The incentive ran both ways: the more my team won, the more stock they had to sell; the more they sold, the more they wanted to help win the next one. On the largest accounts, dedicated on-site resource carried the heavy lifting directly with the client, with the local branch network still driving incremental footfall and leads alongside it. League tables made the results visible, and visibility did the rest: branches that had never proactively passed on a lead started doing it on their own initiative.

Lanes, Not Chaos

Alongside the pairing, I set clear rules for who approached whom, the coverage model that made forty individual relationships behave like one business. National PLC developers, whose regional boundaries usually crossed several of my BDMs' patches, were coordinated centrally, approached Regional Sales Director to Sales Director, timed around their half-year and full-year reporting, so the business spoke to them with one voice instead of several. SME developers were owned directly by the local BDM who knew them best. The handful of true national relationships came to me or my direct reports personally. Wherever an account sat, the standard of service behind it was now the same.

A Land Team, Not a Land Grab

The old approach to winning an instruction was, in effect, a pitch: "can we sell your houses, we're quite good at it", a relationship sell that depended on luck or persistence. I built a small land team whose only job was sourcing land opportunities and bringing them to preferred developer partners before anyone else could. Bringing a developer an opportunity they didn't already have changes the entire conversation: it's no longer a favour I'm asking for, it's one I'm owed. That reciprocity is what won the sales-agency appointment on the homes eventually built there, and it opened cross-sales the old cold-pitch approach never could.

Built the Layer, Rather Than Hired It

Regional sales managers on the largest accounts were recruited from inside the business and grown into the role, each running a number of sites and reporting directly to me. That was deliberate. Internal promotion gave me people who already understood the operating model, cut ramp time to near zero, and created a visible progression route that made the roles underneath easier to fill and easier to keep. Building a management layer is slower than stretching one person across dozens of live sites. It is also the only version that survives the person who built it.

Result

c.£1.1bn of GDV under contract across a 4,000-unit pipeline, built through forty designed relationships rather than forty individual efforts. The commercial effect was not only volume. Pipeline became forecastable for the first time, which changed what the division could credibly commit to at budget, and the cost of winning fell because an agency network of c.250 offices started generating leads it had never previously bothered to pass on. It kept producing after I stopped holding it together personally, which is the only real test of whether it was ever a system. For the client, the change was just as visible: a national housebuilder now dealt with one commercial position and one service standard wherever they operated, rather than negotiating separately with four parts of the same company.
