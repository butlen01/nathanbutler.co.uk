BDM Structure

A BDM Structure Built to Scale, Local to National

Forty business development managers. Forty estate agency regions I didn't manage. Five new-homes divisions I did. This is how those three worlds were aligned into one structure — and one £1.1bn pipeline.

£1.1bn GDV pipeline, contracts secured

Situation

Business development managers were aligned purely by geography. On paper that looked sensible — until you saw what it actually produced. A BDM could approach any developer about anything within their patch, so national and regional housebuilders whose operations crossed several patches ended up hearing from several parts of my business at once: different pitches, different fees, sometimes a materially different service, all supposedly from the same company.

Underneath that sat a bigger gap. My BDMs worked alongside a network of estate agency branches that would ultimately need to sell whatever stock we won — but the two functions had almost no formal alignment. Success depended on how entrepreneurial an individual BDM happened to be that quarter, not on anything the business itself had built. It wasn't a strategy. It was forty people improvising in the same general direction.

40 Business development managers, one for every agency region
40 Estate agency regions — entirely outside my reporting line
5 New-homes divisions — the business that did report to me

The Leadership Challenge

The real difficulty wasn't designing a better process. It was that the two populations who needed to work as one — my BDMs and the estate agency regional managers — sat in entirely different parts of the business, under entirely different leadership, with no shared reporting line to fall back on. I couldn't instruct forty regional managers to prioritise my BDMs. I had to make it worth their while to choose to.

So I aligned the two structures directly: every BDM was paired one-to-one with the agency regional manager covering the same patch — forty pairings in total, each one a genuine business relationship rather than a geographic coincidence. Two people, two functions, one shared incentive to grow the same patch together. That pairing, not a memo and not a mandate, is what made the rest of the structure work.

Approach

The Win:Sell Model

I built the commercial logic that made the pairing worth it. My team won the instruction; their branch sold it — off the back of buyers and sellers their agents were already meeting every day, a route to market I could never have replicated by building my own network from nothing. The incentive ran both ways: the more we won, the more stock they had to sell; the more they sold, the more they wanted to help us win the next one. On the largest accounts, dedicated on-site resource carried the heavy lifting directly with the client, with the local branch network still driving incremental footfall and leads alongside it. League tables made the results visible, and visibility did the rest — branches that had never proactively found us a lead started doing it on their own initiative.

Lanes, Not Chaos

Alongside the pairing, I set clear rules for who approached whom. National PLC developers, whose regional boundaries usually crossed several of my BDMs' patches, were coordinated centrally — approached Regional Sales Director to Sales Director, timed around their half-year and full-year reporting, so the business spoke to them with one voice instead of several. SME developers were owned directly by the local BDM who knew them best. The handful of true national relationships came to me or my direct reports personally. Wherever an account sat, the standard of service behind it was now the same.

A Land Team, Not a Land Grab

The old approach to winning an instruction was, in effect, a pitch: "can we sell your houses, we're quite good at it" — a relationship sell that depended on luck or persistence. I built a small land team whose only job was sourcing land opportunities and bringing them to preferred developer partners before anyone else could. Bringing a developer an opportunity they didn't already have changes the entire conversation: it's no longer a favour I'm asking for, it's one I'm owed. That reciprocity is what won the sales-agency appointment on the homes eventually built there — and it opened cross-sales the old cold-pitch approach never could.

Result

None of this shows up as a single clever idea — it shows up as a structure that kept working after I stopped personally holding it together. Forty BDM/regional-manager pairings, coordinated lanes for national, regional and SME developers, and a land team turning opportunity into obligation, built a c.£1.1bn GDV pipeline sourced through relationships designed to repeat, not luck designed to run out.
