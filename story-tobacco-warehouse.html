The Tobacco Warehouse: Seven Years to Win the Biggest Mandate the Business Ever Landed

The world's largest brick-built building, standing on Stanley Dock beside the site of Everton's new stadium at Bramley-Moore Dock. A seven-year pursuit, through planning, redesign and constant reconfiguration, for the single largest instruction the business had ever seen on offer.

500+ Units the single largest instruction the business has ever won, at Stanley Dock

Situation

Some buildings are simply the crème de la crème, and the Tobacco Warehouse is one of them. Built in 1901 on Liverpool's Stanley Dock, it is the largest brick-built building on earth: fourteen storeys, 1.6 million square feet, 27 million bricks, a Grade II-listed structure so vast and so photogenic it has become one of the city's most filmed backdrops, most recently as the beating heart of Netflix's House of Guinness. It sits a few hundred metres from Bramley-Moore Dock, where Everton were building their new stadium, in a corner of the docklands that was rapidly becoming the most watched regeneration story in the city.

Converting it into apartments was never going to be simple, and it wasn't meant to be won quickly. The instruction on offer, once it finally landed, was for 500-plus apartments on a fee scale that made it the largest single mandate the business had ever landed, before a single pound of attachment sales income or the publicity value of being named on the most talked-about address in Liverpool. It was, without argument, the single largest mandate the business had ever had the chance to win in one go. Getting it meant surviving seven years of a process most people would have walked away from in the first eighteen months.

Staying in the Room for Free

A landmark instruction like this doesn't move through a single pitch meeting. It moves through years of planning committees, revised massing, reconfigured unit mixes, and a developer testing every agent in the room long before any instruction is actually signed. That testing has a name in the trade, and it isn't flattering: being bled for information. Strategic input, market data, pricing logic, sales-strategy thinking: all of it handed over meeting after meeting, year after year, with no guarantee any of it would ever convert into a fee.

The choice was to stay anyway. Every meeting attended, every request for insight answered properly rather than guarded, backing the relationship on the belief that being consistently useful for free was the only route to being trusted with the instruction when it was finally ready to be placed. Competitors circled throughout, some for a year, some for two, and dropped away when the return on their patience didn't show up fast enough. Seven years is a long time to keep believing a building will eventually be yours to sell.

Courting Dublin

The developer behind Stanley Dock's regeneration is Irish, headquartered in Dublin, and the people who mattered most to the pursuit weren't only in Liverpool: they were the developer's own principals, wealthy and understandably guarded about who they let close to a building of this significance. Winning their confidence meant multiple trips to Ireland, sitting across the table from people used to being courted by everyone and impressed by very few of them, and doing it repeatedly over years rather than once, well, in a single pitch.

That relationship was the actual instruction being won, long before a single apartment was ever marketed. A building this iconic was never going to be handed to whoever pitched hardest on the day. It was going to go to whoever the developer's own people trusted enough, after years of contact, to put their name on Liverpool's most significant sales instruction.

Two Markets, One Sales Strategy

Winning the instruction was only the start of the commercial problem. A building this large needed a sales strategy that could run two fundamentally different markets at once: overseas investors, drawn to a landmark asset with a story attached to it, bought through international roadshows and repeat trips to present the opportunity directly; and domestic buyers, moving through a very different journey entirely: mortgage-dependent, price-sensitive, and reliant on government schemes like Help to Buy to make an off-plan purchase in a heritage conversion actually affordable. Running both simultaneously, without either undermining the other on price or pace, was its own piece of design work, built specifically for a building where the two buyer types would ultimately be living side by side.

Commercial Outcome

The single largest mandate the business has ever landed. 500-plus apartments, plus attachment sales income and the publicity value of being named on the city's most recognisable address. Seven years of staying in the room, being useful without being paid for it, and repeated trips to Dublin to earn trust the pitch deck alone was never going to buy.

Customer Outcome

Two entirely different buyer journeys designed to run side by side without either undermining the other: overseas investors buying a landmark asset, and domestic, mortgage-dependent buyers relying on government schemes to make an off-plan heritage purchase achievable.
